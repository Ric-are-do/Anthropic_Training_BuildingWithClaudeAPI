# Chaining Workflows

Chaining workflows might seem obvious at first, but they're actually one of the most useful patterns you'll encounter when working with Claude. This approach becomes especially valuable when dealing with complex tasks or long prompts that Claude struggles to handle consistently.

---

## What is Workflow Chaining?

A chaining workflow breaks down a large, complex task into smaller, sequential subtasks. Instead of asking Claude to do everything at once, you split the work into focused steps that build on each other.

**Practical example:** imagine building a social media marketing tool that creates and posts videos automatically. Rather than asking Claude to handle everything in one massive prompt, break it down like this:

```
User request: "Create and post a trending video"
        │
        ▼
Step 1: Find related trending topics on Twitter
        │  (external API call — no Claude needed)
        ▼
Step 2: Claude selects the most interesting topic
        │
        ▼
Step 3: Claude researches the selected topic
        │
        ▼
Step 4: Claude writes a script for a short format video
        │
        ▼
Step 5: AI avatar + text-to-speech generates the video
        │  (non-LLM processing)
        ▼
Step 6: Post the video to social media
        │  (external API call — no Claude needed)
        ▼
      Done
```

Each step feeds its output into the next. Claude only handles the steps it's good at — selecting, researching, and writing. Non-LLM steps (API calls, video generation) sit in between without disrupting the flow.

---

## Why Chain Instead of One Big Prompt?

You might wonder why not combine all the Claude tasks into a single prompt. The key benefit is **focus** — when you give Claude one specific task at a time, it can concentrate on doing that task well rather than juggling multiple requirements simultaneously.

The chaining approach offers several advantages:

- **Split large tasks** into smaller, non-parallelizable subtasks
- **Optionally do non-LLM processing** between each step (API calls, data transformation, validation)
- **Keep Claude focused** on one aspect of the overall task at a time

---

## The Long Prompt Problem

Here's where chaining becomes really valuable. You'll often encounter situations where you need Claude to produce content with many specific constraints. For example, you want Claude to write a technical article that must:

- Not mention that it's written by an AI
- Avoid using emojis
- Skip clichéd or overly casual language
- Write in a professional, technical tone

Even with all these constraints clearly stated in one prompt, Claude might still violate some of them. You might get back an article that uses emojis, mentions AI authorship, or sounds unprofessional. The more constraints you stack into a single prompt, the more likely some get ignored.

---

## The Chaining Solution

Instead of fighting with one massive prompt, use a two-step chaining approach:

```
Step 1: Generate
  Prompt: "Write a technical article about X"
        │
        ▼
  Claude produces a first draft
  (it might violate some constraints — that's okay)
        │
        ▼
Step 2: Revise
  Prompt: "Revise the article below. Follow these steps:
           1. Identify any location where the text identifies
              the author as an AI and remove them
           2. Find and remove all emojis
           3. Locate any cringey writing and replace it with
              text that would be written by a technical writer"
        │
        ▼
  Claude focuses entirely on fixing issues
  (no content creation — just targeted revision)
        │
        ▼
  Final polished article
```

This works because Claude can focus entirely on the revision task rather than trying to balance content creation with constraint adherence at the same time. Two focused steps reliably outperform one overloaded prompt.

---

## When to Use Chaining

Chaining workflows are particularly useful when:

- You have complex tasks with multiple requirements
- Claude consistently ignores some constraints in long prompts
- You need to process or validate outputs between steps
- You want to keep each interaction focused and manageable

While chaining might seem like extra work, it often produces better results than cramming everything into a single prompt. The key is recognising when a task is complex enough to benefit from being broken down into focused, sequential steps.
